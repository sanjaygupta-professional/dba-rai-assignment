SLIDE 2 — EXECUTIVE SUMMARY1:00

Here's the one slide a board needs. Our payer handles six hundred fifty thousand prior authorizations a year. About three hundred ninety thousand need a licensed nurse to review them. The problem isn't judgment — it's that most of a nurse's time goes to gathering evidence, not deciding. That's four point one million dollars a year of licensed labor spent on assembly work, not decisions. CareBridge is a decision packet, grounded in the patient record: for every criterion, met, unmet, or uncertain, with every claim linked to its source. The AI can recommend approve, pend, or route. It cannot deny. Our target is a thirty-five percent cut in nurse touch-time, with a kill line below twenty-five percent, set before we saw any results. The ask today: a thirty-five-thousand-dollar, four-week prototype, to de-risk a one point six million dollar build.

SLIDE 3 — A1 RECAP (CONTEXT / PROBLEM / STAKEHOLDERS)~47s

Quick recap of Assignment One, since everything today builds on it. Same context: a US healthcare payer, prior authorization, six hundred fifty thousand requests a year. The problem: reviewer time goes to assembling evidence, not judgment — four point one million dollars a year. The stakeholders: the UM nurse, the medical director, the member waiting for care, the regulator, and the executive funding the build. Our hypothesis was simple — cut touch-time by thirty-five percent, with a kill line under twenty-five percent, committed before we saw a single result. A prototype is not a product. It is disposable. Its only job is to test the business case before anyone commits real money.

SLIDE 4 — THE WORKFLOW~43s

Here's the workflow. A case arrives — referral, notes, claims. Deterministic rules check eligibility, codes, and network first — that's rules, not AI, because rules are cheap, auditable, and cannot hallucinate. Then the AI reads the evidence, matches it to policy, cites the source, and builds the packet: approve, pend, or route. The nurse decides — she checks the citations, and that is the one step we are compressing. Anything routed goes to the medical director, who owns it, including every denial. An audit log runs underneath every step. The AI touches three steps in the middle. Every step with authority over the outcome stays human.

SLIDE 5 — EXPLAINABILITY: FIVE STAKEHOLDER “WHY”S~44s

Explainability here is not one thing — it's five different questions, from five different people. The nurse asks why a criterion is met, and gets the exact source line. The medical director asks why a case landed on his desk, and gets the open question, not a score. Compliance asks whether we can defend a decision, and gets the full audit trail. A member asks why they were pended, and gets one named missing item — not a form letter. An executive asks whether the system is behaving, and gets groundedness and touch-time at the population level. Same system. Five different answers, built for whoever is asking.

SLIDE 6 — EXPLAINABILITY DEEP-DIVE: TAXONOMY~53s

One layer deeper. On the data side, pinning every answer to a specific policy version is our data lineage. On the model side, we chose ante-hoc, not post-hoc — we did not bolt an explanation onto a black box after the fact. We built the reasoning to be visible from the start: retrieval, plus a citation for every criterion. On the results side, the packet itself is the explanation. Every status is cited, and it carries its own counterfactual: what would flip a pend to an approve is exactly one named missing item, nothing vaguer. If the model reads a conflict in the notes, it flags conflict and routes the case. If a policy version is missing, it stops and asks a human — it never guesses.

SLIDE 7 — SYSTEM DESIGN: ONE PIPELINE, THREE WORKERS1:14

Thank you, Sanjay. I will show how the system is built. Think of it as one pipeline, with three kinds of workers. First, rules. They check eligibility, codes, and network. This covers sixty to seventy percent of cases. No AI here — rules are fast, and they never guess. Second, the AI. It reads the clinical notes. It matches each note to a policy rule, and it must cite where it found the answer — every claim needs a source, every time. Third, a checker. We call it the groundedness gate. It checks every AI claim against the real document, word for word. The bar is ninety-five percent or higher. If a packet does not pass, it does not go to a nurse — it goes to a queue instead. No guessing, ever, at any step. Around this whole pipeline sit guardrails that apply to every case, all day: PHI stays protected, the AI can never deny care, and the policy version is always pinned to one edition. These rules sit above the pipeline like a ceiling — nothing passes under them unchecked.

SLIDE 8 — CPU VS GPU: THE LATENCY BUDGET1:07

Next question: CPU or GPU? People often pick GPU out of habit. We looked at the actual time budget first. Normal prior-authorization turnaround is three and a half days. Our packet is ready in under one hour. That is a huge amount of spare time — we do not need instant answers. So the rules stay on cheap, simple hardware. The AI reasoning runs on CPU, in one big batch, overnight, while everyone is asleep. This is a batch job, not a live chat, and that one fact changes almost every choice after it. We only use a GPU in one place — on-prem, for the embedding step — because that is where patient data cannot leave the building. It runs about ten percent of one GPU, most of the time idle. Paying for instant GPU speed here would buy nothing nobody asked for. Nobody is waiting at a screen for this answer. The hardware choice follows the deadline, not the other way around.

SLIDE 9 — FERMI SIZING~52s

Let's size this out loud, quickly. Three hundred ninety thousand cases a year is about fifteen hundred cases a day. That is not a large number for a batch job — it fits comfortably inside one overnight run. Now compare the cost per case. Nurse labor costs about ten dollars fifty per case. The AI costs about seventeen cents per case — a small fraction of the labor cost it is helping to reduce. The GPU adds very little, under ten percent of one machine, running overnight for embeddings only. So the AI is cheap, and the pipeline is small enough to run on ordinary hardware. The expensive part was always the nurse's time, and that is exactly what we are trying to give back to her.

SLIDE 10 — DEPLOYMENT ARCHITECTURE: PHI ZONE / MANAGED ZONE1:05

Now, where does this actually run? Two zones, one clear line between them. On one side, the PHI zone. This is on-prem, inside our own walls. It holds intake, the patient data, and the one GPU for embeddings. On the other side, the managed zone. This is cloud. It holds the batch scheduler, the AI model, the policy library, and the audit log. Only one thing ever crosses that line: a de-identified summary. Never a name, never a raw note, never a chart. PHI is masked on our own servers, before any call reaches the AI. And every decision, every packet, gets written to an audit log we cannot edit — kept for seven years, beyond the six years HIPAA requires. That boundary is the whole security story in one sentence: nothing identifiable ever leaves the building. It is really just two rooms and one guarded door, and only a summary ever walks through that door.

SLIDE 12 — RAI: PRODUCTIVITY WITHOUT REPLACEMENT1:16

Thank you. No handoff needed — the video just finished, so I will pick up from here. My part is responsible AI. First question: does this help the nurse, or replace her? It has to help, not replace, by design, and I want to explain why we can say that with confidence. Today, a nurse spends time gathering evidence for each case. The packet does that gathering for her. Her job becomes checking and deciding — the part that needs a real clinical mind, not a keyboard. The AI can never deny a case. Only a licensed clinician can deny. That rule is not just written in a policy document — it is built into the software itself. There is no deny button for the AI, anywhere in the system, for anyone to press. Nurses also help design the packet. Every packet has a button for a reviewer to say, this is wrong, and that feedback becomes training data for the next version. Any time saved is given back as capacity, never turned into a quota for more cases per hour, and never used to cut headcount.

SLIDE 13 — RESPONSIBILITY, PER STAKEHOLDER1:03

We looked at responsibility for each person in this system, one at a time, the way the class taught us to. For the people who build it: the risk is learning bad habits from old, biased decisions. So old denial data is used only to measure speed, never to judge what is fair, and anything unusual gets flagged for a deeper fairness check before it is trusted. For the medical director: does he still own every denial? Yes. Structurally, yes — the AI has no path to deny, so that authority never quietly slips away from him. For the member: are they safer, or worse off? We watch the override rate closely, with a ceiling of five percent. If a nurse or doctor overturns the AI's packet more than that, it is a warning sign, and we stop the rollout to look closer. There is no automatic denial of care. Ever, under any condition.

SLIDE 14 — SECURITY: THE PIPELINE, INSTRUMENTED~57s

On security: every safeguard sits exactly where the risk enters, not bundled together at the end. At intake, we screen for prompt injection — a clinical note should never be able to secretly tell the AI what to do. Before any data reaches the AI, PHI is masked, on our own servers, inside our own walls. Inside the pipeline, the groundedness gate blocks any packet under ninety-five percent — no packet reaches a nurse unless every claim in it is checked against the source. If the policy version is missing or wrong, the system stops and sends the case to a human, instead of guessing at which rule applies. And in the background, we watch for drift, bias, and unusual traffic, all the time, every day. Each safeguard sits right where that specific risk would first enter the system.

SLIDE 15 — STILL A PROTOTYPE? YES — CLOSE1:05

So — is this still a prototype, even though it looks finished? Yes. All the data is synthetic. No real patient data touched this system, at any point. It's built to be thrown away — the effort went into the explanation, the citations, the audit trail, because that is what we are actually testing, not the engineering underneath it. It still answers a real question: does the packet make review faster, and can a nurse trust it enough to act on it without double-checking everything herself. It deliberately leaves out scale and live integration — those belong to the MVP build, priced separately, not to this four-week test. We are asking for three things. Approve the next stage, on the condition these prototype tests pass. Fund a proper safety and fairness study before any real pilot begins. And let us run a vendor search to firm up build costs. That is CareBridge. Thank you — we welcome your questions.
