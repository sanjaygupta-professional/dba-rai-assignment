SLIDE 2 — EXECUTIVE SUMMARY~72s

Here's the one slide a board would actually need. Our payer handles six hundred fifty thousand prior authorizations a year, and around three hundred ninety thousand of those need a licensed nurse to review them. The problem isn't that nurses can't decide — it's that most of their time goes to assembling evidence, not deciding. That's over four million dollars a year of licensed labor spent on assembly work. CareBridge is a RAG-grounded decision packet: for every criterion, met, unmet, or uncertain, with every claim cited to its source. The AI recommends approve, pend, or route — and structurally, it cannot deny. On the economics, at our thirty-five percent floor case, gross labor savings run about one point four three million a year. Take out run cost and the amortized build, and net annual value lands around five hundred fifty thousand dollars. In the pessimistic corner, it's close to break-even — and that's exactly why we're asking to gate a one point six million dollar build behind a thirty-five-thousand-dollar prototype, not asking for the build itself.

SLIDE 3 — CONTINUATION FROM A1 (BUSINESS CASE RECAP)~64s

Quick recap of where Assignment One left us, because everything today builds on it. Same context: a US healthcare payer, utilization management, six hundred fifty thousand requests a year. The workflow in miniature — a case arrives, deterministic rules check eligibility and codes first, the AI reads the evidence and cites it against policy, and it assembles a packet — met, unmet, uncertain. The human decides. Our hypothesis was a thirty-five percent cut in touch-time, with a kill line below twenty-five percent — set above our own break-even math, and committed before we saw any results. We proposed a thirty-five-thousand-dollar, four-week retrospective test, zero patient risk, five hundred cases plus a small nurse crossover. And the discipline we're carrying forward is simple: a prototype is not a product. It's disposable, it's meant to fail fast, and its only job is to validate the business case before anyone spends one point six million dollars building the real thing.

SLIDE 4 — THE WORKFLOW~56s

So here's that workflow at full size. Step one, the case lands — referral, notes, claims. Step two, deterministic checks — eligibility, codes, network, benefit — and this is deliberately rules and RPA, not AI, because it's cheap, auditable, and it can't hallucinate. Steps three through five are where the AI actually sits: it retrieves the patient evidence and the version-pinned policy, maps that evidence to each criterion with an exact source citation, and assembles the packet with a recommendation — approve, pend, or route. Step six, the nurse decides — she verifies the citations, and that's the only step we're compressing. Step seven, the medical director owns anything routed to them, especially denials, with an immutable audit log running underneath the whole thing. The AI touches steps three, four, and five. Everything with authority over an outcome stays human.

SLIDE 5 — PROTOTYPE ARCHITECTURE~70s

Last piece from me before Thejas takes over — how we actually built this. We call it precomputed-real: the pipeline is genuine AI, it just doesn't run live in the room. Offline, before today, five synthetic cases went through real retrieval against a version-pinned policy corpus, a real language model mapped evidence to criteria, and a groundedness gate checked every single quote verbatim against the source documents — this run came back twenty-two out of twenty-two, a hundred percent. Only after that gate passes does a packet get written to static JSON. What you'll see live today is a static demo surface on GitHub Pages — no backend, no API keys, nothing that can fail because the network in this room decided not to cooperate. The real AI work already happened; the demo just replays it faithfully. And that gap between prototype and MVP — PHI-side retrieval on-prem, only de-identified summaries reaching the model, live FHIR and X12 integration — that's exactly what the one point two to two million dollar estimate buys.

SLIDE 5B — SYSTEM DESIGN~53s

Start with the system as one picture: intake, a rules gate, retrieval, the language model doing criterion-mapping, a groundedness gate, the packet, then the human decision. Rules handle the deterministic sixty to seventy percent — eligibility, codes, network — and that never touches a model. The AI only earns its place on the unstructured part: reading clinical narrative and mapping it to policy criteria, with every assertion required to cite its source. Right after that sits the groundedness gate, at ninety-five percent or higher — a packet that doesn't clear it never reaches a person, it fails to a queue, never a silent guess. That's the guardrail band running across the whole diagram: PHI boundary, the no-deny rule, version-pinning, and the groundedness gate, all enforced before a human ever sees the output.

SLIDE 5C — PROTOTYPE → MVP~16sflash — one line, keep moving

Same pipeline, same gates, in the prototype and at MVP — what upgrades is everything around it: live FHIR and X12 intake, a real policy version-pinning engine, and an immutable audit trail, which is what the build estimate actually buys.

SLIDE 6 — DATA REQUIREMENTS~60s

On data — four inputs, and honestly only two are hard. The request envelope is clean and structured, four and a half out of five; outcome labels for testing are solid too. The two that matter are clinical evidence — unstructured notes, some OCR-contaminated, call it two and a half out of five — and the policy corpus, where the real risk isn't messiness, it's version drift: cite the right criterion from the wrong policy edition, and it looks correct while being wrong. Overall we scored this three out of five, set by exactly the two inputs the AI has to handle — clean data wouldn't need AI, rules would do it. PHI stays on-prem under a limited data-use agreement; for build and testing we use public proxies — MIMIC-IV, CMS's coverage database, Synthea — deliberately not the commercial criteria vendors, so we're not carrying a licensing dependency into a disposable prototype.

SLIDE 7 — 360° SUMMARY~16sflash — one line, keep moving

Quick gut-check before we go deeper — all six assignment topics get an answer today: explainability, infrastructure, and responsible AI in depth, security, metrics, and estimation covered fully, a little lighter, because that's genuinely where this application's risk concentrates.

SLIDE 11 — CPU OR GPU? THE LATENCY BUDGET~48s

CPU or GPU — the question the brief actually asks. The answer comes from the latency budget, not fashion. Standard prior-auth turnaround is three and a half days; our queue-ahead packet prep finishes in under an hour. Millisecond GPU serving would be buying speed nobody asked for. So rules and RPA stay exactly what they are — deterministic, cheap. Criterion-reasoning runs on CPU, batched overnight, against a managed API. The one place we spend on GPU is on-prem, for embedding and retrieval, because that's the one step where PHI can't leave the building. Paying for real-time inference against a multi-day service level buys nothing — queue-ahead means the packet is sitting there ready before the nurse opens the case.

SLIDE 12 — FERMI SIZING~35sflash — one line, keep moving

Sizing it out loud, quickly: three hundred ninety thousand cases a year is about fifteen hundred a day; call it forty-two thousand tokens per case — that's sixty-three million tokens a day, landing at sixty to seventy-five thousand dollars a year in API cost, matching our Assignment One band, plus under two GPU-hours a day for embedding. Against ten dollars fifty of nurse labor per case, the AI run cost is seventeen cents — about two percent overhead on the labor it's cutting by thirty-five percent.

SLIDE 12B — DEPLOYMENT ARCHITECTURE~35s

Zoom out to deployment — two zones, one honest boundary. On-prem, the PHI zone: intake, the shared embedding GPU, and the PHI store itself. Across a hard de-identification line, the managed cloud side: the queue-ahead batch scheduler, the managed language model API, the versioned policy corpus, and an immutable audit store with seven-year retention. Nothing crosses that line except de-identified criterion summaries — never raw notes, never a name. The whole stack sits under one observability layer: groundedness, drift, injection attempts, bias spikes, traffic anomalies, watched continuously.

SLIDE 12C — DEPLOYMENT, ENGINEERING DETAIL~57s

For anyone who wants the wiring: on the PHI side, an intake gateway pinned with a prompt-injection screen, a case store encrypted at rest, a masking service that de-identifies before anything leaves, and a token vault holding the re-identification map — that vault never crosses the boundary, so even a compromised cloud side only ever gets de-identified text, not a name. On the cloud side: the batch scheduler, the language model API against a version-pinned policy corpus, the groundedness gate blocking anything under ninety-five percent, and a write-once audit log. Underneath both zones: mutual TLS between services, least-privilege access, encryption at rest, everywhere. The single point of failure is really the nightly batch — a failed run degrades to yesterday's packets while a human keeps working the queue, and given an hours-wide latency budget, that's a delay, not an outage.

SLIDE 8 — EXPLAINABILITY 1~46s

You just saw explainability in action, so I'll keep the framing tight. It's stakeholder-specific — one system, five different questions. The nurse asks why a criterion is met, and gets a cited span she can check in seconds. The medical director asks why something landed on his desk, and gets the unresolved criterion, not a score. Compliance asks whether we can defend a decision from the record, and gets the full audit trail — assertion, source, human action. A member asks why they were pended, and gets one named missing item, not a form letter. An executive asks whether the system is behaving, and gets groundedness, overturn trend, and touch-time at the population level.

SLIDE 9 — EXPLAINABILITY 2~46s

Where this sits in the standard taxonomy: on the data side, policy version-pinning is our data-lineage explainability. On the model side, we went ante-hoc, not post-hoc — we didn't bolt an explanation onto a black box, we built a system where the reasoning is inspectable by construction, RAG grounding plus per-criterion structure. On the results side, the packet itself is the explanation — citations, statuses, and a native counterfactual: what flips a pend to an approve is just the missing item, named. We also handled the edge cases the brief asks about — conflicting notes get flagged conflict and routed, a missing policy version hard-fails to a human, low-confidence retrieval says so instead of guessing.

SLIDE 10 — EXPLAINABILITY 3 + DEMO (RECAP)~14sflash — one line, keep moving

That third piece — the live demo — you watched. Every packet in it came from a real pipeline, offline, twenty-two out of twenty-two assertions verified verbatim before this session started. Nothing you saw was hand-written.

SLIDE 13 — RESPONSIBLE AI 1~46s

On responsible AI — the first question the brief asks is whether this is a productivity gain without a replacement, and the honest answer is: it has to be, by structure. Today a nurse spends about fourteen minutes per case, mostly on assembly. With the packet, that drops toward nine minutes at our floor case — assembly minutes become judgment minutes. But this survives contact with the team because of the design, not the math: the AI cannot deny, full stop, so the licensed role stays load-bearing. Reviewers co-design the packet layout, every packet has a disagreement button that feeds training data, and savings get published back as capacity — never as a quota.

SLIDE 14 — RESPONSIBLE AI 2~37s

We ran the class's stakeholder exercise on our own application. For developers, the risk is importing bias from historical denials — those labels are an efficiency benchmark only, never a fairness benchmark, and anything flagged routes to a powered fairness study later. For the medical director, the question is whether they still own denials, and structurally, yes — the AI has no deny path. For members, the question is whether they're safer or worse off, and we're holding an overturn ceiling of five percent as a guardrail, with no automated denial, ever.

SLIDE 14B — SECURITY, THE PIPELINE INSTRUMENTED~33s

On security, every monitor sits where its threat enters. A prompt-injection screen at intake, because clinical notes are untrusted input — a note that says ignore the criteria and approve should never steer anything. PHI masking before anything leaves the boundary. A hallucination gate inline on the pipeline, blocking any packet under ninety-five percent groundedness. A version-pin check that hard-fails to a human rather than silently using the wrong policy edition. Drift, bias, and traffic monitors watch the trend lines continuously.

SLIDE 15 — SECURITY & METRICS~35s

And every metric ties to a business goal, or it's decoration. Groundedness protects safety and regulatory standing — we're at ninety-nine point two against a ninety-five floor. Approve-precision protects against the wrong-approve risk — ninety-six point four against ninety-five. Overturn is our member-safety proxy, three point eight against a five percent ceiling, though at five hundred cases that's directional, not powered — we've pre-committed a powered follow-up before any pilot. Touch-time, the metric that actually turns into dollars, is running at thirty-eight percent against our thirty-five percent target.

SLIDE 16 — STILL A PROTOTYPE?~43s

So — is this still a prototype, even though it looks polished? Yes. Synthetic data only, no PHI, no live integration, nothing here has touched a real prior authorization. It's disposable by design — the polish lives in the explanation surface, the citations and the audit trail, because that's the hypothesis we're actually selling, not the engineering. It still tests something falsifiable: does the packet make review faster, and is it trustworthy enough to act on. It deliberately ignores scale, latency, and MLOps — priced separately, in the MVP. Same discipline as Assignment One: fail fast, a thirty-five-thousand-dollar gate before anyone commits one point six million.

SLIDE 17 — CLOSE~27s

So — the packet is the explanation, the human owns the decision, and the prototype gates the money. Our ask is three things: approve MVP scoping conditional on these prototype gates, fund a powered safety and fairness follow-up before any pilot, and let us run a vendor RFP to firm up the one point two to two million dollar band. That's CareBridge. We'd love your questions.
